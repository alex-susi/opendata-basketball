Screen Coverage Model

A transformer model that uses player tracking data to predict how the defense will guard on-ball screens.

  • A probability for each of five coverages every 0.04 s, from 2 s before the screen to 1 s after
  • Built only from what has already happened at each moment
  • Compared throughout with a multinomial logistic baseline
10 tracked games 1,463 screens graded 5 coverages held out by game past-only inputs, tested live since 2026-11-15

The question

Can we predict how the defense will react to an on-ball screen in the moments leading up to it?

  • SkillCorner's picks table labels both screen defenders on every on-ball screen
    • Ball handler's defender (bhrDefType)
      • Over the screen
      • Under the screen
      • Switch
    • Screener's defender (scrDefType)
      • Show
      • Drop
      • Switch
  • Together they form one of five coverages
  • Ice (14 screens) and blitz (4) are too rare to learn and are left out for now
  • A useful model could help tell a ball handler, scout or film room how the screen will be defended, and how early that is readable

Movement to a coverage

Five stages inside the transformer, from player histories to five probabilities

1 · in10 players × 9 moments16 measures per player at now, 0.2 s ago … 1.6 s ago
2 · each playerLooks backover his own last 1.6 s, sliding, turning, holding his level
3 · togetherPlayers comparedthe four in the screen take in where the other six are
4 · readScreen four + 9 numberstime to act, tonight's tendencies, closeness, closest-approach memory
5 · outThree questionsswitch? if not, under or over? show or drop? → five coverages

Grading

Held out by game, and weighted toward the moments a ball handler can still use

  • Leave one game out · each of the 10 games is predicted by models trained on the other nine
  • The transformer trains on 8 of them and stops training on the 9th (validation game)
  • 1,463 screens with all four screen players tracked from −2 s to +1 s
  • Every frame from −2 s to +1 s is predicted. The main curve decides how much each frame counts

The main curve

How much each frame counts toward a score, by time from the pick frame

  • Why weight frames at all · a plain average over −2 s to +1 s rewards the wrong moments
    • Early frames · the screen is not yet coming, nobody could read the coverage
    • Late frames · the coverage is already on the floor, reading it is hindsight
  • The curve keeps full weight from when the screen is obvious to when the handler comes off it, the window where a read changes his decision
    • −1.8 s · handler and screener start closing on each other
    • −0.9 s · within 15 ft and staying there, the screen is obvious
    • +0.24 s · closest approach, the handler comes off the screener's shoulder
    • +0.5 s · about a quarter-second of reaction time later
  • Each screen's weights sum to 1, so every screen counts once
  • Used three ways
    • Stopping training on the validation game
    • The headline score (main curve column in Results)
    • The recall and precision matrices

Why the rare coverages are missed

ReasonEvidence
A switch looks like over
until the screen
  • 71% of switches are read as an over coverage
  • Switch caught 6% at −2 s, 22% at −0.4 s, 31% at the pick frame, 43% at +1 s
  • Even a second after the screen, most switches are still read as over
The top-coverage rule
favours common coverages
  • Dividing each probability by its coverage's share in the other nine games before taking the largest
  • Switch caught 36% (was 21%), under + show 46% (was 19%)
  • Precision falls to 33% and 23%, accuracy to 50% (was 55%)
  • So part of the miss is the rule, and the rest is a weak signal
The signal is there,
just weak
  • Average switch probability 0.23 on real switches, 0.13 on the rest
  • A real switch outranks a random other screen 76% of the time (AUC 0.76)
Game plans vary
  • Switch share by defending team runs from 1% (Valencia) to 33% (Murcia)
  • The same team changes between games · Zaragoza 11% and 23%, Breogan 3% and 17%
  • Tonight's tendencies help a little · switch caught 15% over a defense's first 10 screens, 22% after its 30th
Personnel the model
never sees

  • The model catches 17% of those guard-screen switches

Screens left out

33 of the 1,496 five-coverage screens are not graded

  • 30 have a screen player untracked early in the window (from −2 s for 1 to 44 frames), tracked from then on
  • 3 lose tracking after +0.8 s because the possession ends within a second of the screen. Dropping these uses what happens later
  • All 33 are tracked at the pick frame. File 03_outputs/evaluation/left_out_screens.csv

Limits

What to keep in mind before trusting a read

  • Small sample. Ten games, about 1,170 training screens per held-out game
  • Vendor labels and positions. The coverages come from SkillCorner, and a staff might label some differently
  • The screen is known. The model is told which four players are in the screen. A live system would also need to detect the screen coming
  • When a coverage becomes readable. Tonight's tendencies assume 1 s after its pick frame, a judgment call
  • Drift after the screen. The read slips back after about +0.5 s (log loss 0.73 at +0.4 s, 0.87 at +1 s). Training stops on moments up to +0.5 s
  • Percentages honest from about −0.5 s on. Checked in 15_calibration.R
    • Earlier, the leading coverage is stated too high (8 points at −2 s, 5 at −1 s)
    • After the pick frame, about 5 points too low
    • A confident switch read (60%+) before the pick frame comes true only about a third of the time
  • Rare coverages mostly missed. Switch 21% and under + show 19% caught by the top coverage
  • Attention explains little. Attention is spread almost evenly across the ten players

Logistic baselineIts formula, which inputs it relies on when predicting a game it never saw, and which way each input pushes
The model
  • At every frame from −2 s to +1 s, 26 numbers about the screen → a probability for each of the five coverages
  • Multinomial logistic regression, over + show as the reference coverage
P(y=k∣x)=exp(βk0+βk⊤x~)∑j=15exp(βj0+βj⊤x~),βover + show=0 x~f=xf−μfσf,x∈ℝ26

The 26 inputs

  • 11 four-player measures (distances, levels, how fast the levels change, the screener's distance from the hoop, time to act)
  • Logs of the defense's five readable coverage shares so far
  • Closeness c=1/(1+dhs/5)
  • 9 of the measures × closeness, so a position can count more as the handler reaches the screen without the model being told the time

Fitting

  • Means μf and spreads σf from the training games only
  • Maximises the weighted log-likelihood minus an L2 penalty (nnet decay = 1)
  • Every frame weighted 1/76, so each screen counts once however many frames it has
  • One fit per held-out game, on the other nine
Which inputs it relies on
  • Each held-out game predicted twice, as is and with one input group shuffled across the game's rows
  • Shuffling breaks that input's link to the coverage while keeping its values realistic
  • Rise in log loss = how much the model relies on that input
  • A measure and its "× closeness" twin shuffled together. The five coverage shares are one group
  • Five shuffles per group, averaged. Mean over the 10 games, 95% interval from resampling games (first column)

Live transformer mathEvery tensor, weight matrix and formula from the raw measures to the five probabilities, and how it is trained

Notation

  • B · number of prediction rows in a batch
  • p = 1…10 · a player (1 handler, 2 screener, 3 handler's defender, 4 screener's defender, 5-7 other attackers, 8-10 other defenders)
  • t = 1…9 · a history step (1 = now, 9 = 1.6 s ago)
  • d = 32 · width of every hidden vector
  • All shapes from R/seq_transformer.R and the saved networks

1 · Inputs and standardisation
  • Each prediction row is a tensor X∈ℝ10×9×16 (players × steps × measures) and a context vector c∈ℝ9
  • The 16 measures · x, y, position relative to the screener and to the handler, distance from the hoop, level, velocity, speed, speeding up, turning, level changing, time held at a level, present
  • First, y-type columns multiplied by −1 when needed so the screener is on the y ≥ 0 side at the prediction frame
  • Then each measure standardised with the training screens only
x~p,t,f=xp,t,f−μfσf,c~m=cm−μmσm
  • μf and σf taken over present player-frames of the eight training games
  • Mirrored columns keep μf=0 so their sign survives
  • "Present" keeps μ=0,σ=1
  • A missing player has raw zeros and present = 0
2 · Embedding · measures to 32-wide vectors
hp,t(0)=Wex~p,t+be+st+rρ(p) We∈ℝ32×16,st∈ℝ32(t=1…9),rρ∈ℝ32(6 roles)
  • A linear map turns each player's 16 measures at one step into a 32-wide vector
  • st · learned vector saying which step it is (now, 0.2 s ago, …)
  • r · learned vector saying which role the player has
  • ρ maps players 5-7 to "other attacker" and 8-10 to "other defender", so those six share embeddings and their order does not matter
3 · One attention layer (used three times)
  • Takes n rows H∈ℝn×32 (9 steps of one player, or 10 players), returns rows of the same shape
  • 4 heads of width dk=32/4=8
  • Normalises before each part ("pre-norm") and adds each part back onto its input
Z=LN(H),Qj=ZWjQ,Kj=ZWjK,Vj=ZWjV(j=1…4,32×8each, with biases) Aj=softmax(QjKj⊤8)∈ℝn×n,O=[A1V1∣…∣A4V4]WO H′=H+O,H″=H′+ReLU(LN(H′)W1+b1)W2+b2 LN(u)=γ⊙u−u‾su2+ε+β,W1∈ℝ32×64,W2∈ℝ64×32

In words

  • Each row asks a question (query), every row offers a label (key)
  • The softmax of their dot products gives weights that sum to 1 across the rows
  • The row receives that weighted average of the rows' values
  • Each of the 4 heads can look for something different. WO (32 × 32) mixes them back together
  • A small two-layer network then works on each row alone
  • During training, dropout (rate 0.2) zeroes a random fifth of the attention weights and of each part's output
4 · Attention along time, then across players
up=[Layertime(hp,1:9(0))]t=1∈ℝ32,U=[u1;…;u10]∈ℝ10×32 U′=Layerplayers(2)(Layerplayers(1)(U))∈ℝ10×32

Along time

  • One layer with its own weights, shared by all ten players
  • Each player's 9 steps attend to each other, a 9 × 9 weight matrix per head
  • Only his "now" row is kept. It now carries a summary of his last 1.6 s (sliding, turning, holding a level)

Across players

  • Two layers, each with its own weights
  • The ten "now" rows attend to each other, a 10 × 10 weight matrix per head
  • The Model Attention toggle shows the last layer's weights for rows 1-4, averaged over heads
  • No row is masked, so every player sees every player

  • Sees only the four screen players' rows (which already took in the other six through attention) and the nine context numbers
  • Three scores = three yes/no questions · switch? if not, did the ball defender go under? did the big show?
  • The five probabilities always sum to 1
  • Each defender's own coverage follows by adding
    • Big shows =(1−psw)psh
    • Guard goes under =(1−psw)pun
  • The two defenders share psw, and under + show uses the same pun and psh as every other coverage, so the rare joint coverages borrow strength from the common ones
