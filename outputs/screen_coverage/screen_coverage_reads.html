Screen Coverage Reads

Can tracking data tell a ball handler how an on-ball screen will be defended, and how early? This page sums up the models built so far, what they show, how to improve them, and how a staff could use them.

The question

On every tagged ball screen SkillCorner records how both defenders played it: the ball handler's defender (over, under, switch) and the screener's defender (show, soft, switch). Together they make one call. Ice (14) and blitz (4) are too rare to learn yet and are left out of the models.

What we learned

Movement history pays

Giving the model each player's last 1.6 s cut log loss before the screen from 1.325 to 1.276, in all 10 held-out games, and lifted the top call at −0.4 s from 51% to 55% right. It is never told when the screen comes.

The big tips it early

Before the screen the screener's defender is read right 59% of the time (habit alone: 52%). The ball defender's over-or-under choice still can't beat "expect over" (68% vs 67%) until contact.

Ice has a signature

Ice comes on screens set on the screener's middle shoulder near the sideline, and from four defenses. Even so, a rule built on it would be wrong most of the time. Blitz has no pattern in four cases.

Geometry adds nothing

Screen location, angle, side of the floor, re-screens and the clock did not help the main five calls: the defenders' own positions already carry them.

What we built

  1. Snapshots at four moments

    A logistic model on the four players of the screen (distances, levels and how fast the levels change) at −0.8, −0.4, 0 and +0.4 s around the screen tag. Adding the other six players as raw coordinates made it worse.

  2. A prediction at every frame

    76 models, one per frame from −2 s to +1 s, on the 1,464 screens tracked over that whole window. Player archetypes didn't help. A "hindsight test" showed the early read barely depends on knowing exactly when the screen lands.

  3. Transformers on all ten players

    Every player as a token, with attention letting the four screen players draw on the other six. The version never told when the screen comes became the first live model.

  4. Rare calls, screen geometry and the clock

    EDA on when ice and blitz happen, a test of screen geometry and re-screens (no gain), and time to act (the shot clock or game clock, whichever runs out first) in place of the shot clock in every model.

  5. Time-aware transformer with two heads

    Each player's last 1.6 s in nine steps: position, path relative to the screener and ball handler, speeding up or slowing down, changes of direction, how fast and how long a defender has held his level, plus a memory of how close the handler and screener have come. Three linked outputs (switch; over or under; show or soft) build the five calls, so rare combinations borrow from common ones. Now the live model.

  6. In the play viewer

    The Coverage Read card shows each defender's call separately (on the ball: over, under, switch; on the screener: show, soft, switch) plus the most likely combination, with the tagged call ticked once the screen is set. A Model Attention toggle rings the players the model mixes in. Probability strips show each defender's read beside their raw positions.

The shoulder alone ranks an ice above another screen 87% of the time, but ice is 1% of screens: flagging every middle-shoulder screen within 15 ft of a sideline catches 4 ices among 30 screens. Blitz (4 screens) has no pattern, and season-wide no ball handler is blitzed on more than 1.5% of his picks. Both stay out of the models for now; a separate ice detector built on the shoulder, the sideline and the defense is plausible.

Limits to keep in mind

  • Small sample. Ten games, about 1,300 training screens per held-out game.
  • Vendor labels. The coverage calls are SkillCorner's tags, not a staff's.
  • The screen is known. Every model is told which four players are in the screen, which is only certain afterwards. A live system would also need to detect the screen coming.
  • Drift after the screen. Even with its memory of contact, the live model's read slips back after about +0.5 s, when the timing-aware logistic model is far better.
  • Attention is not an explanation. The model's attention is spread almost evenly across the ten players, so the attention rings show little.
  • What 55% means. It is a lean toward the likely coverage, not a prediction to bet on.

How to improve it

Model structure

  • A longer history (2-3 s) or an explicit "the screen has happened" detector, to stop the drift after contact.
  • A transformer on the four screen players only, to separate the architecture's gain from the other six players' gain.
  • An ice detector as a separate output once more games are available.

Training and tuning

  • A small search over width, layers, heads, dropout and learning rate, inside the training games only.
  • More random starts, averaged; temperature scaling so 70% means 70%.
  • Class weights if catching switches and under + show matters more than overall scores.
  • Pretraining on all tracking frames (for example, predicting where players move next) before learning the calls.

New inputs

  • Ball position and height.
  • Personnel threat: the screener's pop rate and the handler's pull-up rate, built without leaking the season's own outcomes.
  • Explicit help-defender measures, such as distance to the roll lane and the nearest tagger.

Making it live and checking it

  • A screen-detection step ("a screen is coming, from this teammate") so the card switches on without the tag.
  • Results by team, screen location and shot clock, plus calibration curves.
  • A test on new games, since ten games may not carry over to other matchups.

Using it with a staff

Scout

  • Coverage tendencies by context: which defenses ice side screens, how often the big drops against roll men.
  • The earliest tell for each team, such as "their big sits 5 ft below the screener at the step-up, so expect drop".

Coach and film room

  • The strips and the live card show the moment each defender's coverage becomes readable, the basis for clip libraries of each cue.
  • Self-scouting: if the model reads our big's coverage at −0.8 s, we are tipping it and can disguise or delay the commitment.

Ball handler

  • Read the big first: whether he is up at the level or sitting back is visible before contact.
  • "Expect over, read your man's hips at contact." The guard's choice is not readable earlier, so cue cards should not pretend it is.

In-game, later

  • Today, broadcast tracking and after-the-fact tags rule out real-time use; the defense's calls so far can be summarised at timeouts.
  • With live tracking and a screen detector, the card could run during play.

Suggested next steps

  1. Film-check the 12 screens with the new strips, which now show each defender's read on its own.

  2. Fix the drift after the screen with a longer history or a contact detector.

  3. Calibrate and tune with a small search inside the training games.

  4. Add screen detection if a truly live version matters.

  5. Revisit ice as its own detector when more games are available.
