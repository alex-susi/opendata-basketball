Screen Coverage Reads

Can tracking data tell a ball handler how an on-ball screen will be defended, and how early? This page is the project's running record: where things stand, what the live model sees, what it shows, how it was checked, what comes next, and a dated log of every change.

10 tracked games 1,496 tagged screens · 1,463 scored 5 coverage calls every result held out by game past-only inputs, tested live: time-aware transformer, v2, graded on the main curve

Current status

As of 2026-11-15. The live model uses only what had happened by the moment of each prediction (the v2, past-only rules), a test confirms it, and it is on the play viewer's Coverage Read card. Since 2026-11-15 it is graded mostly on the moments a ball handler can use: training stops on a fixed time-weight curve (full weight from 0.9 s before the pick frame until the handler comes off the screener's shoulder) and four random starts are averaged. It is compared with one baseline only: a logistic model that, like it, is never told when the screen comes. The lane experiment, a two-step time-to-screen model and a hoop-speed input were tested and not adopted.

The live model watches the ten players move, the way a coach would on film, and every 0.04 s gives a probability for each of the five calls. What we learned

Movement history pays

Against a logistic model that is also never told when the screen comes, reading each player's last 1.6 s is better by 0.058 before the screen (8 of 10 held-out games) and by 0.141 after it (all 10).

No leak was doing the work

Rebuilt so that nothing after the prediction moment can reach it, the live model scores 1.278 before the screen (was 1.276) and 0.760 after it (was 0.761). Both changes are well inside the noise.

Tonight's habits still count

Counting only the coverages readable so far, with no other game's data, the defense's earlier calls improve the logistic baseline before the screen by 0.038 (95% interval 0.011 to 0.065; better in 8 of 10 games).

The big tips it early

Before the screen the screener's defender is read right 58.5% of the time (habit alone: 51.5%). The ball defender's over-or-under choice barely beats "expect over" (67.5% vs 67.0%) until contact, as in the Fall–Marcos example above.

Helpers barely tip the big

Telling the model how far each off-ball defender is from the screener's straight path to the rim, and how fast he is closing on it, did not improve the big's forecast at −0.4 s (−0.004, interval −0.016 to +0.011, 6 of 10 games) or −0.8 s (+0.000); two more random starts of the unchanged model move it by −0.007. The reason is mostly a weak link, not that the model already knew: when the nearest helper is within 6 ft of the lane, switches are more common (19% vs 10-14%) and soft less so (38-40% vs 46-52%), but the gap alone predicts the big's call no better than no inputs on held-out games, and adding it to the logistic baseline, which cannot see the helpers, gains only −0.002.

Ice has a signature

Ice comes on screens set on the screener's middle shoulder near the sideline, and from four defenses. Even so, a rule built on it would be wrong most of the time. Blitz has no pattern in four cases.

Geometry adds nothing

Screen location, angle, side of the floor, re-screens and the clock did not help the main five calls: the defenders' own positions already carry them (archived test, before the rebuild).

The pipeline

  2. Movement histories

    02_build_sequences.R. The ten players' measures at every frame from −3.6 s to +1 s, past-only.

  3. Train the live model

    03_train_transformer.R, once per held-out game: the next game validates, eight train; training stops on the validation game's main-curve score and four random starts are averaged (since 2026-11-15). Saves its predictions, the validation game's predictions and the networks.

  4. Evaluate

    04_evaluate.R. Scores against the baseline and against v1, each defender's read, each call, the left-out screens, and probability strips for the film-check screens (screen_coverage/03_outputs/evaluation/).

  5. Test that the future can't change the past

    05_test_past_only.R. Details in the audit section.

  6. Export to the play viewer

What remains on purpose

  • The screen is known. Which four players are in the screen, and that a screen is coming, come from the tag. Every result reads "given that this screen is coming".
  • SkillCorner's own positions. About 1 in 5 positions are estimated by the vendor rather than seen on camera, and their method may use later frames. Our code can only promise its own steps.
  • Which screens are graded. See the next section.

The screens left out

33 of the 1,496 five-call screens are not graded, because at least one of the four screen players is untracked somewhere between −2 s and +1 s. This is not mainly about losing tracking after the screen:

Why left outScreensLooks ahead?
Untracked early in the window (from −2 s for 1 to 44 frames), tracked from then on. 9 of these screens come less than 2 s into the possession.30No: at any later moment it is already known.
Tracking stops after +0.8 s because the possession ends within a second of the screen3Yes: dropping them uses what happens later.

All 33 are tracked at the screen tag, and 1,986 of their 2,508 frames are fully tracked, so the models can predict on most of them. The baseline needs the four players at that frame and 0.24 s before; the live model's present flags already cover missing players, though it has rarely seen a screen player missing.

If they were added, frame by frame: the decision whether to predict would be made from the past only (are the four players tracked now and just before?), which is how a live system would work. It would add the 3 quick-outcome screens, removing the only look-ahead in the selection, and early-possession screens such as drags in transition, which the graded set under-represents. It would barely move the overall scores (2% of screens), and early-window scores would be reported both ways. evaluation/left_out_screens.csv

Logistic baseline: what it leans onThe model's formula, which inputs it relies on when predicting a game it never saw, and which way each input pushes
The model

At every frame from −2 s to +1 s, the baseline turns 26 numbers about the screen into a probability for each of the five calls. It is a multinomial logistic regression with over + show as the reference call:

P(y=k∣x)=exp(βk0+βk⊤x~)∑j=15exp(βj0+βj⊤x~),βover + show=0 x~f=xf−μfσf,x∈ℝ26

The 26 inputs are the 11 four-player measures (distances, levels, how fast the levels change, the screener's distance from the hoop, time to act), the logs of the defense's five readable call shares so far, closeness c=1/(1+dhs/5), and 9 of the measures multiplied by closeness, so that a position can count more as the handler reaches the screen without the model being told the time. The means μf and spreads σf come from the training games only.

Fitting maximises the weighted log-likelihood minus an L2 penalty (nnet's decay = 1). Every frame gets weight 1/76, so each screen counts once however many frames it has. One fit per held-out game, on the other nine.

Which inputs it relies on (held out by game)

For each held-out game, the fitted model predicts that game twice: as it is, and with one input group shuffled across the game's rows, which breaks that input's link to the call while keeping its values realistic. The rise in log loss is how much the model relies on that input. A measure and its "× closeness" twin are shuffled together; the five call shares are one group. 1 · Inputs and standardisation

Each prediction row is a tensor X∈ℝ10×9×16 (players × steps × measures: x, y, position relative to the screener and to the handler, distance from the hoop, level, velocity, speed, speeding up, turning, level changing, time held at a level, present) and a context vector c∈ℝ9. Before anything else the y-type columns are multiplied by −1 when needed so the screener is on the y ≥ 0 side at the prediction frame. Then each measure is standardised with the training screens only:

x~p,t,f=xp,t,f−μfσf,c~m=cm−μmσm

μf and σf are taken over present player-frames of the eight training games; the mirrored columns keep μf=0 so their sign survives, and "present" keeps μ=0,σ=1. A missing player has raw zeros and present = 0.

2 · Embedding: measures to 32-wide vectors
hp,t(0)=Wex~p,t+be+st+rρ(p) We∈ℝ32×16,st∈ℝ32(t=1…9),rρ∈ℝ32(6 roles)

A linear map turns each player's 16 measures at one step into a 32-wide vector. Two learned vectors are added: st says which step it is (now, 0.2 s ago, …) and r says which role the player has (ρ maps players 5-7 to "other attacker" and 8-10 to "other defender", so those six share embeddings and their order does not matter).

3 · One attention layer (used three times)

A layer takes n rows H∈ℝn×32 (9 steps of one player, or 10 players) and returns rows of the same shape. It has 4 heads of width dk=32/4=8, normalises before each part ("pre-norm") and adds each part back onto its input:

Z=LN(H),Qj=ZWjQ,Kj=ZWjK,Vj=ZWjV(j=1…4,32×8each, with biases) Aj=softmax(QjKj⊤8)∈ℝn×n,O=[A1V1∣…∣A4V4]WO H′=H+O,H″=H′+ReLU(LN(H′)W1+b1)W2+b2 LN(u)=γ⊙u−u‾su2+ε+β,W1∈ℝ32×64,W2∈ℝ64×32

In words: each row asks a question (query), every row offers a label (key), and the softmax of their dot products gives weights that sum to 1 across the rows. The row then receives that weighted average of the rows' values. Each of the 4 heads can look for something different; WO (32 × 32) mixes them back together. A small two-layer network then works on each row alone. During training, dropout (rate 0.2) zeroes a random fifth of the attention weights and of each part's output.

4 · Attention along time, then across players
up=[Layertime(hp,1:9(0))]t=1∈ℝ32,U=[u1;…;u10]∈ℝ10×32 U′=Layerplayers(2)(Layerplayers(1)(U))∈ℝ10×32

Along time (one layer, its own weights, shared by all ten players): each player's 9 steps attend to each other, a 9 × 9 weight matrix per head, and only his "now" row is kept. It now carries a summary of his last 1.6 s (sliding, turning, holding a level). Across players (two layers, each with its own weights): the ten "now" rows attend to each other, a 10 × 10 weight matrix per head. The Model Attention toggle shows the last layer's weights for rows 1-4, averaged over heads. No row is masked, so every player sees every player.

The read-out sees only the four screen players' rows (which have already taken in the other six through attention) and the nine context numbers. Its three scores are three yes/no questions: switch? If not, did the ball defender go under? Did the big show? The five probabilities always sum to 1, and each defender's own call follows by adding: big shows =(1−psw)psh, guard goes under =(1−psw)pun. Because the two defenders share psw, and under + show uses the same pun and psh as every other call, the rare joint calls borrow strength from the common ones.

The shoulder alone ranks an ice above another screen 87% of the time, but ice is 1% of screens: flagging every middle-shoulder screen within 15 ft of a sideline catches 4 ices among 30 screens. Blitz (4 screens) has no pattern, and season-wide no ball handler is blitzed on more than 1.5% of his picks. Both stay out of the models for now; a separate ice detector built on the shoulder, the sideline and the defense is plausible. (Descriptive, measured at the screen tag; archived test.)

Limits to keep in mind

  • Small sample. Ten games: about 1,320 training screens per held-out game for the baseline, about 1,170 for the live model once its validation game is set aside.
  • Vendor labels and positions. The coverage calls are SkillCorner's tags, not a staff's, and some positions are the vendor's estimates.
  • The screen is known. Every model is told which four players are in the screen, which is only certain afterwards. A live system would also need to detect the screen coming.
  • When a call becomes readable. Tonight's tendencies assume a coverage is known 1 s after its tag. That is a judgment, not something the data states.
  • Drift after the screen. Even with its memory of contact, the live model's read slips back after about +0.5 s (log loss 0.73 at +0.4 s, 0.87 at +1 s; 03_outputs/timing_compare/by_time.csv, "none"). Training now stops on moments up to +0.5 s only, which makes the late drift a little larger (it was 0.72 and 0.84).
  • Honest percentages, mostly. From about −0.5 s on, the card's percentages match how often calls happen. Earlier, its leading call is stated too high (8 points at −2 s, 5 at −1 s), after the pick frame about 5 points too low, and a confident switch read (60%+) before the pick frame comes true only about a third of the time (21 screens from −0.5 s to the pick frame; 15_calibration.R).
  • Attention is not an explanation. The model's attention is spread almost evenly across the ten players, so the attention rings show little.
  • What 55% means. It is a lean toward the likely coverage, not a prediction to bet on.

How to improve it

Model structure

  • A longer history (2-3 s) or an explicit "the screen has happened" detector, to stop the drift after contact.
  • A version on the four screen players only, to separate the architecture's gain from the other six players' gain.
  • An ice detector as a separate output once more games are available.

Training and tuning

  • A small search over width, layers, heads, dropout and learning rate, scored on the pooled held-out main-curve score. Picking per fold on one validation game is unreliable: twice on 2026-11-15 a version won 7 of 10 validation games and showed no test gain.
  • Four random starts are now averaged. Temperature scaling as one number would barely help (1.1143 to 1.1137) and would make the after-screen reads more underconfident; a softening that depends on time from the pick frame fits the calibration pattern.
  • Class weights if catching switches and under + show matters more than overall scores.
  • Pretraining on all tracking frames (for example, predicting where players move next) before learning the calls.

New inputs

  • Ball position and height.
  • Tested 2026-11-15 and not adopted: a two-step time-to-screen estimate (physics or learned; the model already reads timing from movement) and each player's speed toward the hoop (already implied by his distance to the hoop at nine moments).
  • Personnel threat: the screener's pop rate and the handler's pull-up rate, built without leaking the season's own outcomes.
  • Explicit help-defender measures: distance to the roll lane and closing speed were tested on 2026-11-15 and added nothing measurable. The pop (catch space beyond the arc, passing-lane clearance) and drive (direct, screen-side and reject lanes) options passed a logistic screen only narrowly, and the experiment was closed. If revisited: a pass gap that ignores defenders beside the handler, and the screener's shooting threat.

Making it live and checking it

  • A screen-detection step ("a screen is coming, from this teammate") so the card switches on without the tag.
  • Results by team, screen location and shot clock. Calibration curves were added on 2026-11-15 (03_outputs/calibration/).
  • A test on new games, since ten games may not carry over to other matchups.

Using it with a staff

Scout

  • Coverage tendencies by context: which defenses ice side screens, how often the big drops against roll men.
  • The earliest tell for each team, such as "their big sits 5 ft below the screener at the step-up, so expect drop".

Coach and film room

  • The strips and the live card show the moment each defender's coverage becomes readable, the basis for clip libraries of each cue.
  • Self-scouting: if the model reads our big's coverage at −0.8 s, we are tipping it and can disguise or delay the commitment.

Ball handler

  • Read the big first: whether he is up at the level or sitting back is visible before contact.
  • "Expect over, read your man's hips at contact." The guard's choice is not readable earlier, so cue cards should not pretend it is.

In-game, later

  • Today, broadcast tracking and after-the-fact tags rule out real-time use. Tonight's tendencies can be summarised at timeouts, and the model uses them exactly as a bench would: only the coverages already seen.
  • With live tracking and a screen detector, the card could run during play.

Suggested next steps

  1. Decide on the tuning round: a small search over size, dropout and learning rate, picked on the pooled held-out main-curve score (not per fold), with four starts per candidate. Any gain must beat the run-to-run noise of about 0.007.

  2. Fix the early overconfidence: a softening of the probabilities that depends on time from the pick frame, fitted on validation games only, and a look at the confident switch reads before the pick frame that came true only a third of the time.

  3. Film-check the strips in screen_coverage/03_outputs/evaluation/figures/strips/: 12 screens, now drawn with the live v2 model and its own −0.4 s call.

  4. Grade the 33 left-out screens frame by frame, deciding from the past only whether each moment can be predicted; report both versions.

  5. Check the 1 s "readable" assumption: rerun with 0 s, 2 s and "once the trip is over" to see whether the tendency gain depends on it.

  6. Fix the drift after the screen with a longer history or a contact detector.

  7. Add screen detection if a truly live version matters; revisit ice as its own detector when more games are available.
