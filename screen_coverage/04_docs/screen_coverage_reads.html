Screen Coverage Reads

Can tracking data tell a ball handler how an on-ball screen will be defended, and how early? This page is the project's running record: where things stand, what the live model sees, what it shows, how it was checked, what comes next, and a dated log of every change.

The question

On every tagged ball screen SkillCorner records how both defenders played it: the ball handler's defender (over, under, switch) and the screener's defender (show, soft, switch). Together they make one call. Ice (14) and blitz (4) are too rare to learn yet and are left out of the models.

What the live model sees

The live model watches the ten players move, the way a coach would on film, and every 0.04 s gives a probability for each of the five calls. It gets no names, no scouting labels and no warning of when the screen will land. Everything it uses had already happened at that moment. Here is one real moment, then the full list.

What we learned

Movement history pays

Against a logistic model that is also never told when the screen comes, reading each player's last 1.6 s is better by 0.058 before the screen (8 of 10 held-out games) and by 0.141 after it (all 10).

No leak was doing the work

Rebuilt so that nothing after the prediction moment can reach it, the live model scores 1.278 before the screen (was 1.276) and 0.760 after it (was 0.761). Both changes are well inside the noise.

Tonight's habits still count

Counting only the coverages readable so far, with no other game's data, the defense's earlier calls improve the logistic baseline before the screen by 0.038 (95% interval 0.011 to 0.065; better in 8 of 10 games).

The big tips it early

Before the screen the screener's defender is read right 58.5% of the time (habit alone: 51.5%). The ball defender's over-or-under choice barely beats "expect over" (67.5% vs 67.0%) until contact, as in the Fall–Marcos example above.

Ice has a signature

Ice comes on screens set on the screener's middle shoulder near the sideline, and from four defenses. Even so, a rule built on it would be wrong most of the time. Blitz has no pattern in four cases.

Geometry adds nothing

Screen location, angle, side of the floor, re-screens and the clock did not help the main five calls: the defenders' own positions already carry them (archived test, before the rebuild).

The pipeline

  1. Screens, frame rows and the logistic baseline

    01_logistic_baseline.R. The 1,463 screens with all four players tracked from −2 s to +1 s, one row per screen and frame, the four-player measures and tonight's tendencies at each frame, and the baseline: a logistic model never told when the screen comes (with and without the tendencies).

  2. Movement histories

    02_build_sequences.R. The ten players' measures at every frame from −3.6 s to +1 s, past-only.

  3. Train the live model

    03_train_transformer.R, once per held-out game: the next game validates, eight train. Saves its predictions and networks.

  4. Evaluate

    04_evaluate.R. Scores against the baseline and against v1, each defender's read, each call, the left-out screens, and probability strips for the film-check screens (screen_coverage/03_outputs/evaluation/).

  5. Test that the future can't change the past

    05_test_past_only.R. Details in the audit section.

  6. Export to the play viewer

    06_export_live.R. The Coverage Read card shows each defender's call (on the ball: over, under, switch; on the screener: show, soft, switch) and the most likely combination, ticking the tagged call once the screen is set; a Model Attention toggle rings the players the model mixes in.

Earlier work, now in screen_coverage/zz_Archive/: the snapshot model at four moments, the ten-player token transformers and their comparisons, the screen-geometry test, and the logistic models fitted separately at each moment (they know exactly how far away the screen is, a kind of hindsight, so they are no longer a fair comparison). Their findings are in the change log.

The test: the future can't change the past

For 30 screens (20 of them re-screens, where the old rule leaked) at six moments from −2 s to +1 s, 05_test_past_only.R rebuilds every input from the raw data twice: as delivered, and after scrambling everything later than the prediction moment (later positions moved up to 12 ft and 30% deleted, later clocks replaced, the calls of every screen not yet readable replaced at random). It then reruns the live model and refits the baseline.

What remains on purpose

  • The screen is known. Which four players are in the screen, and that a screen is coming, come from the tag. Every result reads "given that this screen is coming".
  • SkillCorner's own positions. About 1 in 5 positions are estimated by the vendor rather than seen on camera, and their method may use later frames. Our code can only promise its own steps.
  • Which screens are graded. See the next section.

The screens left out

33 of the 1,496 five-call screens are not graded, because at least one of the four screen players is untracked somewhere between −2 s and +1 s. This is not mainly about losing tracking after the screen:

Why left outScreensLooks ahead?
Untracked early in the window (from −2 s for 1 to 44 frames), tracked from then on. 9 of these screens come less than 2 s into the possession.30No: at any later moment it is already known.
Tracking stops after +0.8 s because the possession ends within a second of the screen3Yes: dropping them uses what happens later.

All 33 are tracked at the screen tag, and 1,986 of their 2,508 frames are fully tracked, so the models can predict on most of them. The baseline needs the four players at that frame and 0.24 s before; the live model's present flags already cover missing players, though it has rarely seen a screen player missing.

If they were added, frame by frame: the decision whether to predict would be made from the past only (are the four players tracked now and just before?), which is how a live system would work. It would add the 3 quick-outcome screens, removing the only look-ahead in the selection, and early-possession screens such as drags in transition, which the graded set under-represents. It would barely move the overall scores (2% of screens), and early-window scores would be reported both ways. evaluation/left_out_screens.csv

Limits to keep in mind

  • Small sample. Ten games: about 1,320 training screens per held-out game for the baseline, about 1,170 for the live model once its validation game is set aside.
  • Vendor labels and positions. The coverage calls are SkillCorner's tags, not a staff's, and some positions are the vendor's estimates.
  • The screen is known. Every model is told which four players are in the screen, which is only certain afterwards. A live system would also need to detect the screen coming.
  • When a call becomes readable. Tonight's tendencies assume a coverage is known 1 s after its tag. That is a judgment, not something the data states.
  • Drift after the screen. Even with its memory of contact, the live model's read slips back after about +0.5 s (log loss 0.72 at +0.4 s, 0.84 at +1 s).
  • Attention is not an explanation. The model's attention is spread almost evenly across the ten players, so the attention rings show little.
  • What 55% means. It is a lean toward the likely coverage, not a prediction to bet on.

How to improve it

Model structure

  • A longer history (2-3 s) or an explicit "the screen has happened" detector, to stop the drift after contact.
  • A version on the four screen players only, to separate the architecture's gain from the other six players' gain.
  • An ice detector as a separate output once more games are available.

Training and tuning

  • A small search over width, layers, heads, dropout and learning rate, inside the training games only.
  • More random starts, averaged; temperature scaling so 70% means 70%.
  • Class weights if catching switches and under + show matters more than overall scores.
  • Pretraining on all tracking frames (for example, predicting where players move next) before learning the calls.

New inputs

  • Ball position and height.
  • Personnel threat: the screener's pop rate and the handler's pull-up rate, built without leaking the season's own outcomes.
  • Explicit help-defender measures, such as distance to the roll lane and the nearest tagger.

Making it live and checking it

  • A screen-detection step ("a screen is coming, from this teammate") so the card switches on without the tag.
  • Results by team, screen location and shot clock, plus calibration curves.
  • A test on new games, since ten games may not carry over to other matchups.

Using it with a staff

Scout

  • Coverage tendencies by context: which defenses ice side screens, how often the big drops against roll men.
  • The earliest tell for each team, such as "their big sits 5 ft below the screener at the step-up, so expect drop".

Coach and film room

  • The strips and the live card show the moment each defender's coverage becomes readable, the basis for clip libraries of each cue.
  • Self-scouting: if the model reads our big's coverage at −0.8 s, we are tipping it and can disguise or delay the commitment.

Ball handler

  • Read the big first: whether he is up at the level or sitting back is visible before contact.
  • "Expect over, read your man's hips at contact." The guard's choice is not readable earlier, so cue cards should not pretend it is.

In-game, later

  • Today, broadcast tracking and after-the-fact tags rule out real-time use. Tonight's tendencies can be summarised at timeouts, and the model uses them exactly as a bench would: only the coverages already seen.
  • With live tracking and a screen detector, the card could run during play.

Suggested next steps

  1. Film-check the strips in screen_coverage/03_outputs/evaluation/figures/strips/: 12 screens, now drawn with the live v2 model and its own −0.4 s call.

  2. Grade the 33 left-out screens frame by frame, deciding from the past only whether each moment can be predicted; report both versions.

  3. Check the 1 s "readable" assumption: rerun with 0 s, 2 s and "once the trip is over" to see whether the tendency gain depends on it.

  4. Fix the drift after the screen with a longer history or a contact detector.

  5. Calibrate and tune with a small search inside the training games.

  6. Add screen detection if a truly live version matters; revisit ice as its own detector when more games are available.
